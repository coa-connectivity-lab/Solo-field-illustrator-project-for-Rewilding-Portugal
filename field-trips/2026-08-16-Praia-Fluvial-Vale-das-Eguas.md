## Habitat description

The two photos actually taken on this visit aren't of the river pool — they're of a dry, savanna-like grassland set among rounded granite/schist boulders on the high ground above the valley: golden, cured grass studded with wild-oat seed heads (*Avena* sp., cf.), scattered individual holm oaks standing apart from each other rather than forming closed canopy, and big weathered boulders breaking the grass line, all of it reading remarkably like an African-savanna composition rather than typical dense Iberian matorral. That open, scattered-tree structure is itself a management signature: it's the classic dehesa/montado grazing pattern, trees kept apart by grazing and periodic clearing rather than growing into continuous woodland.

What's harder to miss once you look for it: the boulders in both photos are blackened, not their natural grey granite colour, and several of the shrubs and small trees are leafless, spiked, dead-looking skeletons rather than dormant winter growth (this is August). That combination, scorched rock plus standing dead woody growth next to otherwise healthy grass and living oaks, reads as fire scarring, most likely a wildfire from a prior season rather than anything from this visit. Worth treating as a genuine disturbance-history observation for the rewilding record, not just scenery.

The river itself, per the reference photos in the Google Maps gallery rather than my own shots, is a calm, tree-shaded pool on the Côa's main channel: dense bankside alder/willow-type gallery, a small stone-lined channel or leat running alongside the swimming area (likely tied to an old mill or irrigation use, consistent with the region), wooden platforms and a picnic/playground area, and exposed rock and reed clumps breaking the water surface upstream of the pool.

**The Termas do Cró tributary.** A short distance west of the beach, the Côa is joined by the Ribeiro do Cró (also written Ribeira do Cró), a named tributary stream that runs through Termas do Cró, a thermal spa between the parishes of Rapoula do Côa and Seixo do Côa. The springs have a documented history back to Roman-era use, with the first built balneário dating to 1935; its ruins (*Ruínas do Antigo Balneário Termal*, visible on the Google Maps search screenshots saved in this folder) still stand near the current, modern 2011 spa building. This is a real hydrological detail worth having on record for the connectivity survey: the Côa at this point isn't a single channel but a confluence, mainstem plus a smaller, geologically distinct (thermal-influenced) tributary joining it just upstream of the beach.
