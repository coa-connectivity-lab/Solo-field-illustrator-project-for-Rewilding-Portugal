Movimento para a Salvaguarda das Gravuras de Foz Côa — the anti-dam movement, and what it means for the Côa Valley field visits
Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 19 August 2026, from `survey123/Barragem do Pocinho/`

This is desk research, not a field visit: no site was walked for this note. It exists to give proper historical grounding to three site visits already logged this project — `2026-08-17-Praia-Fluvial-do-Coa.md`, `2026-08-15-Coa-Gravures-Penascosa.md`, and `2026-08-15-Coa-vs-Douro-Rivers.md` — each of which touched on "a dam that got cancelled" without the full story. This note is that full story, and a correction to a mix-up worth flagging upfront.

## Two dams, not one — the folder name is misleading

The source folder for this note is named `Barragem do Pocinho`, but the Pocinho Dam and the dam the protest movement actually stopped are two different structures on two different rivers, and it's an easy mix-up to make:

- **Pocinho Dam** is a real, completed, still-operating gravity dam on the **Douro** mainstem, in the municipality of Vila Nova de Foz Côa, built 1974–1982, owned by EDP. Its reservoir backs up roughly 40 km of the Douro. This is the dam behind the still, mirror-flat water photographed in `2026-08-15-Coa-vs-Douro-Rivers.md` — that stillness is Pocinho's reservoir, not the natural Douro. Pocinho was never the target of the rock-art protest movement; it predates the 1994–95 controversy entirely, and it isn't on the Côa at all.
- **The dam the movement actually stopped** was a separate, planned hydroelectric project directly on the **Côa river** itself, at its mouth near Vila Nova de Foz Côa (referred to in Portuguese sources as the Barragem de Foz Côa / Barragem do Côa). This is the one that would have flooded Canada do Inferno, Penascosa, and Ribeira de Piscos, the three rock-art sectors, under a reservoir. Construction was already underway when the engravings were made public in November 1994. It's this project that the 1994–96 civic movement, and this note, is actually about.

The two photos of the Pocinho Dam structure included in the source folder (`A_landmark_(4993879597) (1).jpg`, `Frieiras,_Pocinho_-_panoramio.jpg`) are Wikimedia Commons reference images of the *unrelated, still-standing* Douro dam, not the cancelled Côa one, useful for illustrating what a completed dam on this stretch of river actually looks like, not for illustrating what got stopped.

## Timeline

- **1991** — Nelson Rebanda identifies the first engraved rock (later named Canada do Inferno) near Vila Nova de Foz Côa.
- **Through 1994** — Construction proceeds on the planned Côa dam. A team of Portuguese archaeologists, including Mila Simões de Abreu and João Zilhão, is aware of the growing find but the discovery isn't yet public.
- **November 1994** — The Cavaco Silva government makes the engravings' existence public. SIC television plays a key role in media coverage. On 11 November, international rock-art specialists Paul Bahn, Angelo Fossati and Andrea Arcà begin corresponding about the find through IFRAO (International Federation of Rock Art Organisations), whose Portuguese representative is Mila Simões de Abreu. On 24 November, deputy Eurico Figueiredo, with Deputies António Martinho, Carlos Luís and Rui Vieira, files a formal parliamentary request (*requerimento*) for information from the government. A press conference follows on 28–29 November in the Palácio de S. Bento, calling for suspension of the dam works — from this point the objective of the whole movement never changes: stop the works.
- **Late 1994** — International press picks it up: Paul Bahn and Tim Rayment in the *Sunday Times* (18 December), Marlise Simons in the *New York Times* (27 December). Jean Clottes, president of UNESCO's rock art committee, visits to assess the find.
- **Early 1995** — A local civic movement forms at the Vila Nova de Foz Côa secondary school, led by José Ribeiro, then president of the school's governing council, after a teacher raised the issue with students. The students form a Students' Association (the first the school had ever had) specifically around this cause, and adopt the slogan **"As gravuras não sabem nadar"** ("The engravings can't swim"), adapted from a song called "Não sabe nadar" by the band Black Company. President Mário Soares visits the school on 21 February in solidarity. Other civic groups active alongside the school movement include Côa Vivo, APRIL, and Olho Vivo. The Green Party (Os Verdes) organises "Noites Verdes por Foz Côa" debate nights.
- **19 March 1995** — António Guterres, then PS leader and not yet Prime Minister, visits the Côa Valley and publicly commits to halting the dam if his party wins the upcoming election. He kept that promise.
- **April 1995** — A mega-encampment of students during the Easter holidays draws roughly 2,000 participants. At midnight on 25 April, a separate, peaceful encampment of students, archaeologists and deputies begins outside the Jerónimos Monastery in Lisbon; it runs 70 days, starting with 3–4 people and ending with around 70.
- **Two petitions** circulate: one from the Associação Olho Vivo, ~11,000 signatures, calling for urgent debate in Parliament; a second, organised jointly by school governing/pedagogical councils and the Foz Côa students' association, collects over 70,000 signatures nationally (José Ribeiro, the council president, claimed the true figure ran closer to 200,000).
- **10 June 1995** — Portuguese emigrant communities in the United States, organised as the "Save the Côa Movement" under João Crisóstomo (a former butler to Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis), stage a protest outside the United Nations in New York.
- **October 1995** — The Côa controversy becomes one of the defining issues of the Portuguese legislative election. The PS wins under Guterres.
- **17 January 1996** — Council of Ministers Resolution nº 14/1996 formally suspends construction of the dam.
- **10 August 1996** — The Côa Valley Archaeological Park opens.
- **2 July 1997** — The site is classified a National Monument.
- **December 1998** — UNESCO inscribes the Côa Valley as a World Heritage Site, describing it as "an exceptional concentration of Palaeolithic open-air rock art... the most important known example of the earliest human artistic activity, in this form unique in the world."
- **2010** — The Côa Museum opens; the Fundação Côa Parque is created in 2011 (later dissolved in 2012 and re-established in 2017).

## The part that isn't finished

Stopping construction in 1996 didn't undo what had already been built. A provisional cofferdam (*ensecadeira*), built about 32 m high to dry the riverbed for the main dam's foundations, was never removed. Because it only drains through a small carved outlet, heavy rainfall still backs water up across roughly 10 km of the Côa valley, and it has now submerged 119 of the rock-art panels for a cumulative total of more than 500 days since 1996, with some recent winters seeing over a month of continuous flooding. Demolition of the cofferdam has been on a national list of obsolete river barriers since 2017, but as of the most recent reporting found for this note it had still not happened, estimated cost over €4.5 million and rising, and the lead agency (APA, the Portuguese Environment Agency) declined to comment on the delay.

This matters directly for the connectivity-barriers thread running through the other Côa notes in this project: the actual physical barrier still standing in the Côa today isn't the dam the movement is famous for stopping, it's the abandoned construction debris from that same cancelled project, and it is still doing some of the damage the movement fought to prevent.
