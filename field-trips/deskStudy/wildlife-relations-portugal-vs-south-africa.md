# Wildlife relations, Portugal vs. South Africa — resistance, history, and everyday coexistence

Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 22 August 2026, desk research comparing how Portuguese and South African publics relate to wildlife and conservation. One of six comparative desk studies written in parallel to feed v4 of the eco-connectivity notebook.

This note asks the same question in three registers — active resistance to conservation measures, resistance rooted in historical dispossession, and everyday cultural tolerance of living near wildlife — and compares Portugal's Côa Valley against South Africa's Kruger/APNR landscape in each. The three registers don't map onto each other symmetrically. Two of them do real comparative work; the third, on historical dispossession, turns out to have no honest Portuguese counterpart, and that absence is itself part of what this note is for.

## 1. Resistance to conservation itself

In the Côa Valley, resistance to conservation runs almost entirely through one channel: the wolf. Wolf attacks on livestock rose sharply in the valley between 2012 and 2016, a period when CAP subsidy structures were encouraging extensive, largely unfenced cattle grazing, which made herds easy prey. Cattle rancher Rui Matos's account, reported in *Resurgence*, is representative: his first attack, in 2014, killed a calf worth €500; he waited over a year for government compensation and received about half that value. Frustration of this kind was widespread enough that, in the same period, several of the valley's stable wolf packs simply disappeared — the reporting is careful not to state outright what happened to them, but frames it as farmers "taking matters into their own hands." At a national level this frustration has organised political expression: a Union of Livestock Producers Affected by Wolves has publicly rejected any strengthening of wolf protection unless it comes paired with faster compensation and real prevention support, and Portuguese law's 60-day compensation deadline routinely stretches to one or two years in practice.

Rewilding Portugal's own attitude research on this, published as "From Fear to Favor: attitudes towards wolves south of the Douro, Portugal" (the Côa Valley falls within this study area), interviewed 220 people — 175 general public, 32 livestock owners, 13 hunters — and found general attitudes toward wolves neutral to positive overall, but with a clear split by exposure: livestock owners alone showed negative-to-neutral attitudes with a distinct "not in my backyard" pattern, while the general public and hunters were neutral to positive. Almost half of all respondents said they'd accept living alongside wolves, conditional on reliable compensation and enough wild prey to reduce livestock predation. Rewilding Portugal's own response to the Matos case — 52 guard dogs deployed across valley farms, fencing subsidies, advisory support — reads as a direct answer to exactly the conditions that survey identified.

What I did not find, despite searching specifically for it, is documented organised opposition to Rewilding Portugal's or Faia Brava's land purchases themselves. The reporting instead describes the opposite mechanism: biologists and rewilding organisations buying land parcel by parcel from families who were already abandoning marginal farms and leaving the valley for the cities, not land being taken from people who wanted to keep farming it. Rewilding Portugal's team leader Pedro Prata has described the approach as identifying core areas and "busy acquiring land ownership" within them — an incremental, willing-seller process layered onto a landscape that was depopulating anyway (see Section 2). There is a broader, more diffuse friction worth naming: Portugal's Natura 2000 network now covers roughly 21% of mainland territory and constrains land-use change in Special Areas of Conservation and Special Protection Areas, producing generic tension between the state and private landowners or communal-land (*baldios*) holders. But that is EU regulatory friction at a national scale, not a documented, site-specific protest against rewilding land acquisition in the Côa.

South Africa's version of this resistance is structurally different: it runs through physical boundaries and legal access rather than through compensation for a free-ranging predator on land people already farm. Around 2003, fencing along Kruger's western and eastern boundaries was deliberately removed to open wildlife corridors as part of the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park. Two decades on, that decision is a live source of conflict: communities near Giyani (Mbaula Ranch, Phalakubeni village) reported roughly 60 cattle lost in 2024 alone to predators moving out of the unfenced park and to snares set by illegal hunters inside it, with one farmer reporting over 100 head lost. Recovering strayed livestock is restricted by protected-area and disease-control law — a state veterinarian quoted in the reporting confirmed park authorities can legally seize and destroy animals that wander in, rather than let owners reclaim them — which leaves the loss entirely on the farmer and deepens resentment. The community response includes both formal demands to rebuild the fence and informal fence-cutting, both to retrieve grazing access and, the reporting notes, to enable illegal entry for poaching. This is conflict over a controlled boundary and who may cross it, not over compensation for damage on land already under a farmer's own control — a genuinely different shape of resistance from Portugal's, even though both are, at root, about who absorbs the cost of a large predator's presence.

## 2. Resistance from historical dispossession

South Africa's case here is well documented and specific. The Makuleke community, roughly 1,500 people, was forcibly removed by the apartheid government in 1969 from about 25,000 hectares of land in the far north of what is now Kruger National Park — land bordering Zimbabwe and Mozambique — and relocated to Ntlaveni, near the park's Punda Maria entrance. Their former land was absorbed into the park. This was fortress conservation enacted through the machinery of apartheid removal law, not a voluntary or negotiated process.

The remedy came decades later, through South Africa's post-1994 constitutional land-restitution process. The Makuleke lodged a formal claim in 1995; a settlement agreement dated 30 May 1998 (amended that December) between the community, six national ministries, the provincial government, and South African National Parks restored ownership of the roughly 25,000 hectares to the Makuleke Community Property Association. The community did not move back — they remain at Ntlaveni — but they gained land ownership, tourism-revenue rights, and a joint management board with six members each from the community and SANParks, under a "contractual park" arrangement that commits the land to conservation for a minimum of 25 years, restricts mining, farming, and permanent residence without SANParks' agreement, and gives SANParks right of first refusal if the land is ever sold. It has been described by both sides, and by later academic accounts, as a rare success story within South Africa's broader, often unresolved, land-restitution landscape.

The Côa Valley has no honest equivalent to this, and forcing one would misrepresent both places. Nobody was removed from Côa Valley land by the state to make way for a protected area or a rewilding project. The actual sequence ran the other direction: Portugal's interior experienced a rural exodus beginning in the 1960s, driven by emigration and the pull of urban and foreign labour markets rather than by any conservation policy, and the Côa Valley is among the regions hit hardest — some of the highest rural-abandonment rates recorded anywhere in Europe, with less than 20% of the national population now living in Portugal's interior districts at all. Land was abandoned first, for economic reasons that had nothing to do with wildlife, and rewilding organisations then acquired parcels afterward, from willing sellers, decades after the people who worked that land had already left it. There is no racialised removal apparatus in this history, no forced relocation to make room for a reserve, and consequently no restitution claim, no contractual-park negotiation, and no comparable settlement to point to. If there is a genuine parallel worth drawing, it is a negative one: where Kruger's history is a story of conservation causing displacement that then required a formal justice remedy, the Côa's story is displacement happening first, for unrelated economic reasons, with conservation arriving afterward into land that people had already chosen, however reluctantly, to leave. That is a meaningfully different moral and historical shape, and this note treats it as such rather than papering over the difference for the sake of a tidy three-part structure.

## 3. General coexistence tolerance

Hunting is a major, active, and formally regulated land use across the Côa Valley, and this project's own geospatial data already shows how pervasive it is: the `land_tenure.gpkg` layer built for the eco-connectivity analysis records 337 ICNF hunting-zone polygons within the 30 km Côa Valley study area alone — 194 associative zones (ZCA), 108 municipal zones (ZCM), and 35 touristic/private zones (ZCT). That is not a marginal land use sitting alongside the rewilding landscape; it is one of the densest categories in the entire land-tenure layer, overlapping substantially with the same ground the connectivity survey is mapping. Field visits this project has already logged confirm the pattern directly: on 22 August 2026, Linda photographed a "Zona de Caça Associativa" sign (Processo Nº 465, ICNF) at Largo da Praça, and a "Zona de Caça Turística" sign at Paul de Toirões — the latter standing at the boundary of the very restored wetland site built around the "Water Returns, Life Returns" rewilding narrative (see `2026-08-22-Largo-de-Praca.md` and `2026-08-22-Paul-de-Toirores.md`). Rewilding messaging and active hunting-zone designation aren't segregated into separate parcels of the valley; they sit on the same fences. At national scale, mainland Portugal has roughly 5,000 hunting grounds and around 113,100 licensed hunters, and hunting traditions here run deep and ritualised — the *montaria*, a collective driven hunt on large estates (*coutadas*) explicitly managed for both game and conservation, and *caça a salto*, walked line-hunting for partridge with dogs.

What the attitude research complicates is the intuitive assumption that a strong hunting culture predicts hostility toward a wild predator. In the "From Fear to Favor" survey covering the Côa's own region, hunters were neutral to positive toward wolves, statistically indistinguishable from the general public — it was livestock owners specifically, a smaller and economically distinct group, who carried the negative sentiment. Coexistence tolerance in Portugal, on this evidence, tracks direct economic exposure to predation far more than it tracks a general hunting-versus-wildlife-appreciation divide.

South Africa's coexistence landscape is structured differently again, around who benefits economically from wildlife rather than around a single predator species. Trophy hunting contributes a substantial figure nationally — on the order of $2.5 billion a year, by one widely cited estimate — but the benefit reaching communities living alongside the reserves generating that revenue is disputed and, by at least one 2013 analysis, averaged only around 3% of gross trophy-hunting revenue. Where reserves can generate comparable income through photographic tourism instead — the Timbavati reserve within the Kruger-adjacent APNR system is the cited example, raising conservation levies on photographic visitors specifically — some have shifted the balance away from trophy hunting toward it. For communities directly bordering the unfenced Kruger boundary, though, the lived relationship with wildlife is increasingly framed by loss rather than either hunting heritage or tourism income: cattle killed by predators, crops raided, and since the 2003 fence removal, no reliable boundary between the two land uses at all. That is a coexistence structure built on contested economic benefit-sharing and physical exposure, in a landscape whose reserve boundaries still trace apartheid-era land allocation; Portugal's is built on a single legally protected species, a compensation bureaucracy, and a hunting culture that, on the evidence here, isn't the primary source of anti-predator sentiment it might be assumed to be.
