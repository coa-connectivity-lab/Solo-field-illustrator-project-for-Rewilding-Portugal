Douro vs. Côa: the dam cascade as backdrop to the 1996 cancellation
Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 22 August 2026, desk research for the "Two Rivers, Two Stories" closing chapter sketched in `storyMaps/5-ideas.md`, building on `movimento-salvaguarda-gravuras-foz-coa.md`

`5-ideas.md` sketches this chapter around a figure of "141 dams and weirs" on the Douro and flags it as needing primary-source verification before drafting. That figure doesn't hold up under checking, and the correction turns out to matter for the argument, not just the accuracy. There is no single citable count of "141" for the Douro. There are two real counts at very different scales: a basin-wide inventory of roughly 1,200 dams and weirs across the whole Douro catchment, Spain and Portugal, mainstem and every tributary, most of them small, and a much smaller figure of around 15 major dams on the Douro's own mainstem from its Spanish headwaters to Porto. Both are documented below. Neither is 141. The rest of this note uses the corrected figures and the dam-timeline detail already established in `movimento-salvaguarda-gravuras-foz-coa.md` to ask an open question that note doesn't attempt to answer: whether the Foz Côa protest could plausibly have succeeded in 1996 without the Douro cascade already sitting there as a substitute source of hydroelectric power.

## The Douro cascade, built out before Foz Côa was even proposed

By the early 1980s, roughly a decade before EDP and the Portuguese government signed the 1991 agreement to dam the Côa, the Douro's own mainstem was already a heavily engineered river. Six major hydroelectric dams built in sequence from the 1950s through the early 1980s account for it:

(i) Picote, on the international stretch (Miranda do Douro), built 1953–58, installed capacity 441 MW (Picote I, 195 MW, plus Picote II, added later, 246 MW).

(ii) Bemposta, also on the international stretch (Mogadouro), built 1957–64, installed capacity in the region of 431–439 MW depending on source, combining Bemposta I (240 MW, 1964) and Bemposta II (added 2011).

(iii) Carrapatelo, on the national stretch, built 1964–72, installed capacity 201 MW, and the largest single lock drop on the river at 35 m.

(iv) Régua/Bagaúste, built 1965–73, installed capacity 180 MW.

(v) Valeira, built 1971–75/76, installed capacity 240 MW.

(vi) Pocinho, built 1974–82, installed capacity 186 MW, the dam in the municipality of Vila Nova de Foz Côa itself, already covered in `movimento-salvaguarda-gravuras-foz-coa.md` as the structure that is not the cancelled Côa dam.

Summing just these six gives roughly 1,680 MW of installed hydroelectric capacity already operating on the Douro mainstem by 1982. A seventh dam, Crestuma-Lever, near Porto, came online in 1985 with a further 117 MW, bringing the total to around 1,800 MW, all of it in place before the 1991 EDP-IPPAR protocol that opened the door to archaeological monitoring on the Côa dam site, and years before the 1994 discovery of the engravings turned that monitoring into a national controversy. The planned Côa dam itself, by comparison, was a 136 m structure on a much smaller river, its footprint concentrated on flood-storage and generation rather than on the multi-dam navigation function the Douro cascade also serves. I did not find a published figure for its planned installed capacity in the sources checked for this note; period documentation describes its purpose in terms of regulating flow "para por a funcionar a [central]" (to bring a power station on line) but doesn't state the MW figure in the excerpts available.

Two further points on scale, both corrections to the storyMap draft. First, the "15 major dams" figure: this is the right order of magnitude for named, mainstem dams on the whole Douro from its Spanish headwaters to Porto, roughly five on the Portuguese national stretch (Crestuma-Lever, Carrapatelo, Régua/Bagaúste, Valeira, Pocinho), a handful more on the Spain-Portugal international stretch (Picote and Bemposta among them), and the rest upstream in Spain. Second, the 1,200-dams-and-weirs figure is a different measurement entirely: a 2019 academic inventory (Cortes et al., published in the journal *Water*) counted 1,201 barriers across the entire Douro basin using satellite imagery, comprising 57 dams, 40 mini-hydro installations, and 1,105 medium and small barriers and weirs, spanning both countries and every tributary, with an estimated 25% assessed as obsolete. The two figures answer different questions. Fifteen is "how many dams control the Douro's main channel." Twelve hundred is "how fragmented is the whole basin." The storyMap's 141 doesn't correspond to either.

## Two tourism economies built on opposite outcomes

The Douro's tourism identity is now built directly on the infrastructure the region chose to keep. The Alto Douro Wine Region was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage cultural landscape in December 2001, roughly 24,600 hectares of terraced vineyard across fourteen municipalities, and that listing has anchored a tourism sector with strong institutional backing ever since. River cruise tourism on the navigable Douro, made possible precisely by the lock-and-dam system summarised above, has grown from around 600,000 passengers in 2014, already described at the time as one of the best years the river had seen, to 1,377,858 passengers registered on the Douro waterway in 2024, up 10.6% on 2023's 1,245,314. Stopovers reached 27,916 in 2024, up 190% on 2021. By 2026 reporting, the river's cruise-operator authority had stopped approving new large vessels, a soft capacity ceiling on what is already, by European river-cruise standards, a small but fast-growing market (the Douro still represents only a single-digit share of total European river-cruise capacity, well behind the Rhine and Danube).

The Côa's tourism identity runs on the opposite premise, an intact valley rather than a controlled one. The Vale do Côa rock-art sites, plus the neighbouring Siega Verde sites across the Spanish border, were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in December 1998, two years after the dam was cancelled and the same month the Archaeological Park opened. Visitor numbers are far smaller and access is deliberately restricted, both by site-protection policy and by the Côa's own seasonal flooding: the Parque Arqueológico do Vale do Côa recorded 401,387 total visitors across its first 30 years of operation, from its August 1996 opening through July 2026, an annual average in the low tens of thousands, with 2026 running about 8% ahead of 2025. That is a heritage-tourism model built on scarcity and controlled access to a small number of guided rock-art panels, not a mass-throughput model. It could not be more different in scale from the million-plus-passengers-a-year cruise economy 100 km downriver, and that contrast is itself the material for the chapter this note supports: two rivers a short drive apart, one economy built on damming the river for controlled access by boat, the other built on keeping it wild enough that walking to a rock face still means something.

## The open question this note can't resolve

Here is the question the "Two Rivers, Two Stories" chapter should pose, and the question this desk-research pass can document the backdrop for but not answer: would the 1994–96 anti-dam campaign at Foz Côa have won if the Douro's own hydroelectric capacity hadn't already been built out?

The documented facts support asking the question seriously. By the time the Côa dam was cancelled in January 1996, the Douro mainstem alone was already carrying somewhere close to 1,800 MW of installed hydroelectric capacity, built up over three decades and seven dams, entirely separate from whatever the Côa project would have added. Portugal's electricity sector in the 1990s ran on state-guaranteed power purchase agreements for conventional generation, and EDP was, by multiple accounts, actively expanding hydroelectric capacity through that decade. Cancelling one additional dam on a comparatively small tributary, in a country whose flagship river a few kilometres away was already generating power at that scale, plausibly cost the national grid far less than cancelling would have cost in a counterfactual where the Côa was the only hydroelectric option on the table, or where the Douro cascade didn't exist at all.

But I want to be honest about what I did and didn't find in this pass. I could not locate a published, contemporaneous statement from EDP or the Portuguese government in 1994–96 that frames the Foz Côa cancellation explicitly in terms of "we can afford to lose this because the Douro already covers the need." The academic literature on the case (Coelho and colleagues' "Conflicting values and public decision: the Foz Côa case," among others) treats it as a values conflict between heritage protection and energy/economic development, weighed through environmental impact assessment and multi-criteria decision methods, not as an energy-supply-adequacy calculation with a documented number attached. Nor did I find the planned MW capacity of the Foz Côa dam itself, which would be the other half of any real cost-benefit comparison. Establishing actual causation for a political decision made three decades ago, on the evidence available in a desk-research pass, isn't something these sources support. What the sources do support is the shape of the opportunity: the Douro cascade existed, it was large, and it predated the controversy by a decade. Whether that fact gave the 1996 Council of Ministers political room it wouldn't otherwise have had, or whether the decision would have gone the same way regardless, on the strength of the civic campaign and the engravings' evident scientific importance alone, is the open question. It's worth carrying into the story map as a question the reader is invited to sit with, not a conclusion the desk research has actually proven.
