Portugal's rivers and seas: a national water history, beyond the Côa and Douro
Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 23 August 2026, desk research for the "Water, Empire, and Portugal's Environmental Legacy" closing section of `research/eco-connectivity/notebook/coa_eco_connectivity_story_v5.ipynb`

Everything this project has documented about water so far is Côa and Douro basin material: the Douro's hydroelectric cascade, the cancelled Foz Côa dam, the still-unresolved Barragem de Vale de Madeira question. That is one basin. Portugal's relationship with water is national and maritime, not just one valley's, and this note grounds that wider picture in real hydrological data before the companion notes on ocean currents and colonial ecology build on it.

## Two rivers, two basins, two scales

The Tejo (Tagus) is Iberia's longest river, 1,007 km, with an 80,626 km² basin, of which 24,845 km² lies in Portugal, the rest upstream in Spain. At the Almourol gauging station, downstream of the Spanish border and regulated substantially by the Alcântara Dam upstream, mean annual discharge measured 2006-2019 was 263 m³/s, ranging instantaneously from 0 to 9,874 m³/s. At the Tagus estuary itself, mean annual discharge rises to 440 m³/s, Portugal's second-largest river by that measure ([Frontiers in Marine Science, "The Influence of the River Discharge on Residence Time... for the Tagus Estuary"](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2021.734814/full)).

The Douro, this project's other already-documented river, carries more water: mean discharge around 505 m³/s at its own estuary, ranging from zero to over 13,000 m³/s, per the Douro-estuary hydrodynamics literature already cited in `deskStudy/douro-vs-coa-tourism-dam-counterfactual.md` and `2026-08-15-Coa-vs-Douro-Rivers.md`. That comparison, Tejo second, Douro larger, is itself a small correction to any assumption that the Douro is simply "a" Portuguese river rather than the country's largest.

The Côa, this project's own study river, is smaller than both by an order of magnitude or more, its own published drainage basin around 2,495 km², as established in `research/eco-connectivity/notebook/coa_eco_connectivity_story.ipynb`'s catchment-tracing work. Three rivers, three scales, one country: the Côa is a tributary story inside a much larger national water system, not the whole of it.

## Drought and water scarcity are structural, not incidental

Portugal's National Water Resources Information System, SNIRH (Sistema Nacional de Informação de Recursos Hídricos, https://snirh.apambiente.pt/), run by APA (Agência Portuguesa do Ambiente), is the country's primary hydrological and meteorological monitoring network, gauging river discharge, precipitation, and water levels nationally. Its own documented limitation matters here: SNIRH's monitoring network "is more oriented for flood evaluation and does not allow drought evaluation with a unique classification" ([ISA/CEABN drought-preparedness project brief](https://www.isa.ulisboa.pt/ceabn/uploads/docs/projectos/drought/CS_PORTUGAL_FLYERS)). A country whose national water-data infrastructure is built for floods, not droughts, while regularly experiencing water scarcity severe enough to warrant its own EU-funded adaptation research (the Tagus basin specifically, per [MDPI Water, "Climate Adaptation Needs to Reduce Water Scarcity Vulnerability in the Tagus River Basin"](https://doi.org/10.3390/w14162527)), is a country where drought is a structural condition the monitoring system itself was not originally built to see, not a one-off event.

## The Atlantic side: Portugal as a maritime, not only riverine, nation

Every water story this project has told so far ends at a river mouth. Portugal's own historical identity is built as much on what happens past that point: a small Atlantic-facing country whose 15th-century global reach depended entirely on reading ocean currents correctly, not river flow. The companion note `portugal-land-to-sea-ocean-currents-colonial-routes.md` develops that thread in full, departing from the same estuaries (the Tejo at Lisbon, the Douro at Porto) whose river discharge is documented above.

## What this note does not cover

This is a national-scale framing note, not a comprehensive hydrology of Portugal. It does not attempt a full accounting of the country's dam and reservoir inventory outside the Douro cascade already documented elsewhere in this project, and any national total-installed-capacity figure would need direct sourcing from APA or REN (Redes Energéticas Nacionais) before being cited as fact. That gap is left open rather than filled with an unsourced estimate.
