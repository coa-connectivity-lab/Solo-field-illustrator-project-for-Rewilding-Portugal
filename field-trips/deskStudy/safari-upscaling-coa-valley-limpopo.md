# Safari-scale upscaling: what would it take to move the Côa Valley toward a Limpopo-province-style model?

Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 22 August 2026, desk research combining this project's own eco-connectivity geospatial data with published reserve-size and Greater Limpopo Transfrontier Conservation Area (GLTFCA) literature. One of six comparative desk studies written in parallel to feed v4 of the eco-connectivity notebook; the others cover wildlife relations, rights of nature, the Douro dam counterfactual, renewable energy policy, and integrated infrastructure. This is the last of the six, and the most numbers-heavy: every figure below is either read directly out of this project's own `.gpkg`/`.csv` files with GeoPandas, or cited to a published source, not estimated.

The short answer, stated plainly up front and then worked through: the Côa Valley is not close to Limpopo-province-scale safari tourism, and the gap is not a matter of ambition or money alone. Two of the four barriers below are negotiable over decades (fragmented tenure, thin visitor infrastructure); one is not negotiable in Portuguese or EU law (heritage protection); and the achievable ceiling, even under the most generous assumption, comes out to roughly a tenth of Limpopo's scale, before any land-acquisition realism is applied.

## What's actually on the ground today

Two of this project's own reference tables — `references/ecological-information/data/annual_review_2025_species.csv` (Rewilding Portugal's 2025 annual review) and `visitor_board_species_2026.csv` (site-panel and colleague-confirmed field transcriptions) — give a plain baseline of what the Greater Côa Valley currently holds, as opposed to what a safari-tourism pitch would need.

Present and confirmed: European beaver (first confirmed 2025, natural dispersal from Spain into the Douro/Tormes border area); Sorraia horse (more than 40 individuals recorded in the Ermo das Águias rewilding area as of 2025, including 14 that arrived from Germany that year — the CSV doesn't make clear whether the 14 are additional to or included within the "more than 40," so both readings are left open here); Tauros breeding-back cattle, reported at two different scales in the same source — around 15 individuals released in the Greater Côa Valley in August 2025, against a separate national "Portugal in numbers" infographic figure of 32 semi-wild Tauros, which is very likely a multi-site Rewilding Portugal total rather than a Côa-specific one; red deer and roe deer; cinereous vulture (15 nests, in neighbouring Serra da Malcata); Egyptian vulture, griffon vulture, Bonelli's eagle, golden eagle, black stork; Eurasian otter. And, confirmed by colleague field visit in August 2026 at Ermo das Águias, within the LIFE WolfLux project area: Iberian wolf (*Canis lupus signatus*) — Europe's largest extant native land predator, and a genuinely present, landscape-managed carnivore, not a hypothetical one. This is worth stating clearly because it complicates a lazy version of the next section's argument.

Absent, on the evidence in these same files: anything at the scale of an elephant, rhino, hippo, or African buffalo — Iberia has no living or historically-native equivalent of a multi-tonne ecosystem-engineer herbivore, so there is no version of this landscape, rewilded or not, that produces that guild. And absent beyond the wolf: any second large carnivore. No bear, no lynx appears in either CSV for this area.

## Barrier one: land-tenure fragmentation

`research/eco-connectivity/data/processed/land_tenure.gpkg` holds two layers, read directly for this note. `hunting_zones` (ICNF-regulated Zonas de Caça) has 337 features covering 663,158 ha in total — mean parcel size 1,968 ha, median 1,498 ha, ranging from 2.3 ha to 18,617 ha. Broken out by the tenure categories already established in `wildlife-relations-portugal-vs-south-africa.md`: 194 Associative zones (ZCA, 317,754 ha total, median 1,525 ha), 108 Municipal zones (ZCM, 312,021 ha, median 1,806 ha), and 35 Touristic/private zones (ZCT, 33,383 ha, median 517 ha). `private_reserves` has exactly one feature — Faia Brava — at 214.6 ha in this project's own dataset, built from OSM relation 16062824. That figure is worth flagging rather than taking at face value: the published figure for Faia Brava's actual reserve extent is 856.87 ha (Wikipedia, cross-checked against Rewilding Europe's own reporting), with ATN's own stated expansion ambition at roughly 2,500 ha. The OSM relation this project's acquisition script pulled evidently captures a core parcel, not the reserve's full current footprint — a data-quality gap worth fixing before this figure is quoted elsewhere, and a reminder that "214.6 ha" below means specifically what this project's `land_tenure.gpkg` contains, not the reserve's true size.

What counts as a viable contiguous block for a large-carnivore or large-herbivore reintroduction isn't a number to guess at — it's in the literature. A single wolf pack's territory runs 100–500 km² (10,000–50,000 ha); a documented Lausitz-region cluster of 13 packs held 3,500 km² between them, averaging 300 km² (30,000 ha) per pack (van Maanen, *ECOS* 40(6), 2019). Araújo & Alagador's 2024 *Current Biology* rewilding-potential mapping of Europe goes further and defines "mega-rewilding" ecosystems specifically as contiguous patches exceeding 100,000 ha with minimal human interference — the scale they judge necessary for long-term (>100-year) survival of carnivore and omnivore populations with large home ranges. Applying both thresholds to the computed numbers above: only 6 of the 337 individual hunting-zone parcels exceed 10,000 ha (summing to 81,533 ha), and none individually reach 100,000 ha.

Dissolving every touching or overlapping hunting-zone and private-reserve polygon into contiguous blocks changes the picture, and this is worth doing carefully rather than stopping at the per-parcel count. The 338 combined features merge into 43 contiguous blocks; three of those exceed the 10,000 ha single-pack-territory threshold (601,343 ha combined, 58.8% of the study area), and two clear the 100,000 ha mega-rewilding threshold outright — 471,350 ha and 103,080 ha, together 56.1% of the study area. Geometrically, in other words, most of the Côa Valley's hunting-zone land already sits inside patches large enough for the literature's own bar. But geometric contiguity is not the same as an acquirable or manageable estate: those blocks are stitched together out of dozens of separately licensed ZCA, ZCM, and ZCT concessions under different associations, municipalities, and private operators, none of them for sale as a unit, none of them under common management. The fragmentation barrier here isn't that the land is too small in aggregate — it's that no single actor holds enough of it to run one.

## Barrier two: no Big-Five-equivalent species roster

Covered under "what's on the ground" above, restated as a barrier rather than a baseline: a safari-tourism pitch built on Limpopo's model sells lion, leopard, elephant, rhino, and buffalo — five species chosen historically for difficulty and danger of hunting them on foot, now sold as photographic-viewing spectacle. The Côa Valley's confirmed roster tops out at one genuinely wild, landscape-scale predator (Iberian wolf, present but elusive and rarely seen even by researchers, let alone tourists on a game drive) plus semi-wild grazers standing in for extinct megafauna (Sorraia horse and Tauros cattle as tarpan/aurochs proxies) and a strong raptor/scavenger guild. That is a legitimate and increasingly well-documented rewilding story in its own right — it is not a Big-Five-equivalent tourism product, and no amount of land consolidation changes what species exist to put on it.

## Barrier three: road density and the visitor-infrastructure gap

This project's own `distance_to_road_m.tif` covariate (built for the resistance/suitability modeling, treating roads as a landscape barrier rather than as a public-access asset) records a median distance to the nearest mapped road of 27,564 m and a mean of 33,374 m across its ~1,517 × 862 cell grid; only 4.4% of cells fall within 250 m of a mapped road, rising to just 6.3% within 1 km and 7.5% within 2 km. That statistic describes a resistance-model input, not a tourism-accessibility survey, but directionally it says the same thing a visitor would notice on the ground: this is a low-road-density interior landscape, the opposite of what a safari lodge circuit assumes — internal game-viewing tracks, an airstrip, multiple lodge nodes within drivable range of each other. The project's own `tourism_sites.gpkg` reinforces this from the visitor-infrastructure side: `not_yet_visited` holds 3 points and `potential_ecotourism_sites` holds 6, split across high- and low-connectivity-zone tags. That is the entire currently-mapped universe of ecotourism-relevant sites for this landscape — a handful of trailheads, beaches, and a rock-art reception point, not a multi-lodge circuit.

## Barrier four: land that isn't fragmented, it's legally unavailable

This is a different kind of barrier from the three above, and worth keeping separate rather than folding into "fragmentation," because it isn't negotiable through willing-seller land purchase at any timescale. `research/eco-connectivity/data/processed/heritage_protection.gpkg`, built since the last pass at this question, now holds two layers read directly for this note: `unesco_alto_douro` at 24,599.98 ha — an exact match to the published Alto Douro Wine Region figure (UNESCO, inscribed December 2001) — and `coa_rock_art_core` at 568.62 ha. Intersecting both against `study_area.gpkg`'s real boundary (rather than assuming either sits entirely inside it) gives the actual overlap: 6,615.75 ha of the UNESCO wine-region polygon falls within the 10,230 km² Côa study area (26.9% of that polygon — most of the Alto Douro Wine Region sits outside this project's study boundary, further down the Douro), and all 568.62 ha of the rock-art core does. Combined, real computed heritage exclusion inside the study area is 7,184.37 ha — 0.70% of the study area's total extent.

Both exclusions are hard legal ones, and for different reasons that shouldn't be conflated. The Alto Douro Wine Region's protection rests on its status as a living cultural landscape of terraced viticulture with roughly 2,000 years of winemaking tradition on this ground — but the terraces actually visible today mostly date from a specific late-19th/early-20th-century rebuild, after phylloxera devastated the original pre-1860 *socalcos* and the region replanted on wider, regraded platforms (grafted onto American rootstock). The tradition is ancient; the built landscape UNESCO protects is, in large part, a resilience story roughly 130 years old, not a 2,000-year-old physical artefact — worth stating precisely rather than letting the two collapse into one figure. The Côa rock-art core's protection rests on an entirely different basis: Palaeolithic open-air engravings classified a National Monument in 1997 and inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage in December 1998, the direct legacy of the 1994–96 anti-dam campaign documented in `movimento-salvaguarda-gravuras-foz-coa.md`. Neither zone converts to game-reserve land under any private-purchase model this project could pursue; both are, correctly, out of scope permanently, not just currently.

This isn't only a narrative-doc observation any more. The connectivity model's own resistance surface (`research/eco-connectivity/scripts/build_resistance_surfaces.py`) now folds a `heritage_conflict_penalty` directly onto the Land group's resistance raster — an area-fill penalty rasterized from these same two `heritage_protection.gpkg` polygons, applied uniformly across their full extent rather than fading at the edge the way the surface's separate, point-based `barrier_penalty` (fences, roads, dams, logged via Survey123) decays with distance. The two mechanisms are deliberately different in kind: heritage protection is a legal exclusion that behaves the same at the centre of the zone as at its edge; a fence or a weir is a permeability-graded, spatially localised obstacle. This desk study's barrier-four/barrier-one distinction and the resistance model's heritage-penalty/barrier-penalty distinction are the same finding, reached independently by two different parts of this pipeline.

## What scale is actually achievable

The GLTFCA — Kruger National Park (South Africa), Limpopo National Park (Mozambique), and Gonarezhou National Park (Zimbabwe), plus surrounding conservancies and communal land — measures almost 10 million ha (Peace Parks Foundation; TFCA Portal), confirming the figure already cited in the v3 notebook's Limpopo section. Its fenced core, the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park itself (the three national parks with the internal veterinary fences removed since 2003), is smaller: 3.7 million ha. That fence-removal mechanism — opening corridors between land three national governments already owned — is the model already covered in this project's Portugal-vs-South-Africa comparison in v3, and it is explicitly not available here: the Côa study area doesn't border a national park anywhere near that scale, so there is no equivalent fence to remove. Any Côa upscaling has to run on a private-purchase, willing-seller model instead, the same mechanism Rewilding Portugal already uses for Faia Brava.

Against the 10-million-ha GLTFCA figure, the Côa study area's own computed extent — verified directly from `study_area.gpkg` rather than trusted secondhand — is 1,023,036 ha (10,230.4 km², confirming the v3 notebook's figure independently), or 10.23% of GLTFCA scale. Subtracting the real computed heritage exclusion from this note's own numbers above (7,184 ha) leaves 1,015,852 ha, or 10.16% of GLTFCA scale, as the absolute geographic ceiling — the area left over if literally every hectare of the study area that isn't legally heritage-excluded were somehow assembled under one rewilding tenure. That ceiling is generous to the point of being unreal: 64.8% of the study area (663,158 ha) is currently under active hunting-zone licence across 337 separate concessions, none of it on the market, and this project's actual land holding to date — Faia Brava, per this project's own dataset, 214.6 ha; per the published figure, 856.87 ha — is 0.0021%–0.0086% of GLTFCA scale. ATN's own stated 2,500 ha expansion target, the most concrete acquisition ambition documented anywhere in this project's sources, comes to 0.025% of GLTFCA scale. The realistic trajectory sits far closer to that bottom figure than to the 10% ceiling: a private-purchase, willing-seller process, at the pace this project's other desk research (`wildlife-relations-portugal-vs-south-africa.md`) already documents as parcel-by-parcel and generational, is not a mechanism that closes a two-orders-of-magnitude gap on any planning horizon shorter than decades, if it closes it at all.
