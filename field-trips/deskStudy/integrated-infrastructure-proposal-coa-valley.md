# An Integrated Infrastructure Proposal for the Côa Valley — Solar Access Canopies and Photo-Tourism Lodges

Linda Angulo Lopez, desk study, compiled 22 August 2026

The companion note `renewable-energy-policy-coa-valley.md` lays out the open question this desk study answers: after Rewilding Portugal's public opposition to the Sophia photovoltaic plant near Fundão–Penamacor, does the Côa Valley rewilding movement's position amount to opposing renewable energy altogether, or is there a version of energy infrastructure the movement could actively propose rather than only reject? This note is a concrete answer, not a restatement of the question. It has two parts — a solar proposal and a tourism-lodge proposal — pitched as one integrated package: energy revenue that strengthens the access infrastructure reserves already depend on, and reserve expansion funded by tourism rather than further land conversion.

Both halves below are proposal sketches, not engineering-ready designs or feasibility studies. Every number is labelled as an illustrative order-of-magnitude estimate, not a real project figure.

## Part 1 — Solar canopies over existing reserve access roads

### The siting logic, stated in Rewilding Portugal's own terms

Rewilding Portugal's objection to Sophia was never a blanket objection to solar power. Their public response argued specifically that "there are many other areas that have already been artificialised, abandoned after human use... which would allow the production of this same energy without destroying habitats," and called on government to map artificial land, incentivise panels on public/logistics/industrial roofs, and tie new capacity to a "Nature-based Energy Transition programme" that funds ecosystem restoration rather than displacing it. Quercus, the other Portuguese environmental NGO active on Sophia, made the same argument independently, naming disused mines and existing infrastructure specifically.

An access road is about as artificialised as rural land gets. It's already graded, already cleared of the habitat structure that makes adjacent land ecologically valuable, already carries the visual and acoustic disturbance of vehicle traffic, and it already exists — no new corridor has to be cut through scrub or woodland to site a canopy over it. If the movement's stated principle is "build on what's already disturbed," a reserve's own access track is a stronger fit for that principle than almost any other parcel in the landscape, including rooftops, because it's land the reserve itself already accepts as a working footprint rather than habitat.

### The engineering basis: agrivoltaics, not a road-canopy precedent

No project was found in this research specifically for solar canopies over rural reserve-access roads at this scale — that gap should be stated plainly rather than glossed over. What does exist, well established and heavily documented by DOE and IEA-PVPS, is **agrivoltaics** (dual-use solar): PV arrays mounted 2–4 m above ground on structural supports, spaced to leave enough vertical and horizontal clearance for machinery to pass underneath. IEA-PVPS Task 13's 2025 report on dual land use documents row spacing tuned to implement widths (commonly 6 m or 12 m clearance bays) and mounting heights in the 2.5–5 m range built specifically so farm vehicles can drive beneath the panel plane while the land underneath keeps functioning for its original use.

The proposal here is to apply that same proven canopy technique to reserve access tracks instead of cropland: an elevated PV structure spanning the road, high enough to clear a Land Rover or a maintenance vehicle, running along a stretch of track a reserve already grades and drives regularly. This is not a new engineering problem — it's the same structural solution agrivoltaics already solves, redirected at a different kind of vehicle corridor.

### Candidate sites, from this project's own field data

Three access routes documented on this trip are plausible candidates, in descending order of how well-defined the physical corridor is in the notes so far:

- **Vale Carapito — VC1 trailhead access track, near Vilar Maior.** The 22 August return visit (`field-trips/2026-08-22-Vale-Carapito-Return-Visit.md`) describes the Percurso VC1 loop starting from a dirt track through open holm-oak/broom scrubland — an unpaved, already-graded corridor reaching the trailhead. Vale Carapito's Endangered Landscapes-verified footprint of 656 ha under natural grazing (per `storyMaps/scratch.md`) makes the access track itself a natural place to site a canopy without touching that grazing land at all.
- **Ermo das Águias — the Vale de Madeira access track.** The 23 August desk study (`field-trips/2026-08-23-Ermo-das-Aguias-Desk-Study.md`) records a rural track descending roughly 2 km from Vale de Madeira village to the reserve entrance at Ribeira de Gaiteiros — a defined, single-purpose access corridor connecting an existing settlement (with an existing grid connection, by virtue of being a village) to a roughly 600–1,000 ha reserve.
- **Faia Brava — the approach road near Cidadelhe.** The 23 August visit note (`field-trips/2026-08-23-Faia-Brava.md`) documents a small road bridge crossing the Côa at the valley bottom near a cluster of buildings, with the reserve viewpoint reached from Cidadelhe village (Figueira de Castelo Rodrigo municipality). This is the least well-characterised of the three in the field notes — the note describes the bridge and the clifftop viewpoint but not the access road's surface or width in detail — so it's listed here as a candidate needing a dedicated site visit before it could be proposed with any specificity.

### What would need to be true for this to work

This is a sketch, not a design, and three things would need field verification before it went further:

- **Road width and grading.** A canopy needs footings set back from the running surface without narrowing the track below what service vehicles need, and the substrate needs to bear structural loads — none of that is known from the current field notes, which describe these tracks visually but don't record width, surface type in engineering terms, or grade.
- **Grid connection distance.** Ermo das Águias' village-to-reserve track has the advantage of one end already sitting at an inhabited settlement, which typically means nearby low-voltage distribution infrastructure; Vale Carapito and Faia Brava's connection distance to the nearest existing line isn't established in this project's data and would need a utility-side check, not a desk-study guess.
- **Road corridor ownership and permitting.** Whether Rewilding Portugal, the local municipality (Sabugal for Vilar Maior, Figueira de Castelo Rodrigo for Cidadelhe, Pinhel for Vale de Madeira), or a private landowner controls the road corridor determines who could even propose this, and Portugal's licensing regime for small-scale distributed solar (autoconsumo / self-consumption rules under DL 15/2022, plus any protected-landscape or Natura 2000 overlay given the Côa Valley's UNESCO and IBA designations) would set real constraints not assessed here.

## Part 2 — Photo-tourism lodges as an economic engine for reserve expansion

### The Timbavati model, and where its money actually comes from

Timbavati Private Nature Reserve, part of the Associated Private Nature Reserves (APNR) bordering Kruger National Park in South Africa, covers roughly 53,396 ha and hosts a double-digit number of commercial lodges (reported between 12 and 19 depending on source and year) charging conservation levies on top of accommodation. Current published levy rates (Timbavati's own 2026 schedule) run R660 per adult per night at fully catered lodges, up from R575 in 2024 and R400 in 2020 — a levy that has been rising steadily as security costs, driven mainly by anti-poaching operations, have grown to consume roughly half the reserve's operational budget. Independent reporting (Conservation Frontlines, 2020) put photo-tourism conservation levies at just over 62% of Timbavati's total income that year, with hunting and member contributions making up most of the rest; more recent secondary figures cited elsewhere put the levy share closer to 55%, consistent with a funding mix that shifts year to year with visitor numbers and security spend.

**This distinction has to be stated plainly, not glossed over:** the levy-funded majority of Timbavati's budget comes from photographic tourism, but the *remainder* — the other roughly 35–45% — comes primarily from trophy hunting conducted on some APNR properties, plus reserve-member contributions. The proposal in this note is for the photographic-tourism-plus-conservation-levy piece of that model only. It is explicitly not a proposal to import trophy hunting into the Côa Valley, and any adoption of the Timbavati mechanism should be built with that boundary stated up front, not left implicit.

### Scaling the mechanism down, honestly

Timbavati's absolute revenue figures don't transplant to the Côa Valley — the two landscapes differ by roughly two orders of magnitude. Rewilding Portugal's individual reserves in the Greater Côa Valley run in the 650–1,000 ha range each (Faia Brava ~857 ha, Ermo das Águias ~600–1,000 ha, Vale Carapito ~656 ha under natural grazing), inside a wider 120,000 ha landscape-connectivity corridor Rewilding Europe is working to stitch together. A single Côa reserve is roughly 1–2% of Timbavati's area. Applying Timbavati's lodge density (very roughly one lodge per 3,000–4,500 ha, depending on which lodge count is used) to a 650–1,000 ha Côa reserve implies capacity for a fraction of one lodge, not several — realistically, a single small-footprint lodge (in the range of 6–12 rooms, the scale already typical of boutique rural tourism in this part of Portugal) per expanding reserve, not a Timbavati-style cluster.

### Where in this project's own data the sites for this already are

This project's `research/eco-connectivity/data/processed/tourism_sites.gpkg` already names candidate locations for exactly this kind of development, independent of this proposal:

- The `not_yet_visited` layer lists three sites explicitly tagged to the project's storyMap chapter structure — **Vale Carapito** (Movement chapter), **Ponte de Sequeiros**, and the **Ribeira do Mosteiro / Sernancelhe area** (Connection chapter) — locations the project has already flagged as worth developing further but hasn't field-visited yet.
- The `potential_ecotourism_sites` layer scores six locations by a `connectivity_value` metric and tags each with a `corridor_note`. Three are marked **high-connectivity zone**: the Penascosa rock-art reception (value 1.21), Praia Fluvial de Rapoula do Côa (0.97), and Lageosa, the Sorraia horse site (1.03). A high-connectivity tag is exactly the wrong signal for new built infrastructure — these are sites the project's own data flags as ecologically valuable movement corridors, meaning any lodge siting drawn from this layer should preferentially target the **low-connectivity zone** sites instead (Pontão Manuel José weir, the Nascente do Rio Côa/Estação de Biodiversidade at Foios, and Praia Fluvial de Vale das Éguas), which the data itself suggests are lower-stakes for wildlife movement while still being visitor-relevant locations.

This is the same logic as the solar siting argument in Part 1, run through the tourism lens: use the project's own connectivity data to route new infrastructure toward the land that's already lower-value for movement, not the land the data says matters most.

### Reserve expansion funded by tourism, not land conversion

The pitch is straightforward: a single small lodge sited at a low-connectivity location, charging a Timbavati-style conservation levy on top of room rate, generates a dedicated revenue stream that can be earmarked specifically for expanding rewilding land tenure — buying or leasing the next parcel that extends the 120,000 ha corridor — rather than relying solely on grant funding (Endangered Landscapes Programme, Rewilding Europe capital) that has to be won competitively and isn't guaranteed to renew. It converts visitor demand that's already happening (Rewilding Portugal already runs guided walks at Vale Carapito, Ermo das Águias, Faia Brava and Ribeira do Mosteiro, per `field-notes/Daily_Observation_Practice.md`) into a standing budget line rather than one-off guide fees.

## Rough order-of-magnitude economics — illustrative only, not a feasibility study

These numbers exist to show the two revenue streams are comparable in scale, not to substitute for actual engineering or financial modelling. Do not cite these as project figures.

| | Solar access canopy (per site) | Single boutique lodge + levy (per site) |
|---|---|---|
| Illustrative footprint | ~0.5–1 km of access track, ~4 m wide canopy (≈0.2–0.4 ha of panel area) | 6–12 rooms, ~15–25 guests/night at typical occupancy |
| Illustrative capacity/scale | Roughly 0.3–0.6 MW, based on typical canopy-configuration PV density | 15–25 person-nights/day at a modest ~60% occupancy |
| Illustrative annual output/throughput | ~500–1,000 MWh/yr at mainland Portugal's ~1,700 kWh/kWp/yr yield | ~5,500–9,000 person-nights/yr |
| Illustrative annual revenue | Roughly €30,000–€80,000/yr at recent Portuguese wholesale/PPA solar prices (€60–80/MWh) | Levy alone, at a Timbavati-scaled rate (say €15–25/person/night, well below Timbavati's current rate given Côa's different tourism market): roughly €80,000–€225,000/yr from the levy component alone, before any accommodation revenue |

Read together, the two streams are the same order of magnitude — tens of thousands to low hundreds of thousands of euros per site per year — which is the point of proposing them as one package rather than picking one. Neither generates Timbavati-scale money, and neither should be expected to; at Côa Valley scale, the honest pitch is that this is enough to fund incremental reserve-corridor expansion and access-infrastructure upkeep, not a wholesale replacement for grant-funded rewilding capital.

## The integrated pitch

Put together, this is a specific, adoptable answer to "should the movement oppose all renewables": site solar generation on the reserves' own already-disturbed access corridors, using the exact agrivoltaic canopy technique the solar industry already uses over cropland, so that renewable energy revenue strengthens the infrastructure reserves already depend on instead of competing with them for land. Fund reserve expansion with a Timbavati-style photographic-tourism conservation levy, scaled honestly down to Côa Valley's actual size and routed toward the project's own lower-connectivity sites rather than its highest-value corridors. Both halves apply Rewilding Portugal's own "build on what's already disturbed, not on what's still wild" principle — one to energy, one to tourism infrastructure — which is what makes this one proposal rather than two unrelated ideas bolted together.
