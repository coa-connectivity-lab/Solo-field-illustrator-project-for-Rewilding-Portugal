Renewable energy siting and the Côa Valley: Rewilding Portugal's SOPHIA position, and whether the movement should go further

Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 22 August 2026, desk research for the "If the Price Is Biodiversity, Don't Call It Green" chapter sketched in `storyMaps/5-ideas.md`, reading `references/ecological-information/rewilding-portugal-news-if-the-price-to-pay-is-biodiversity-itself-dont-call-it-green-energy.pdf` directly rather than from memory

This is desk research, not a field visit. It exists to check what Rewilding Portugal actually said about the SOPHIA solar project against what the storyMap chapter concept assumes they said, and then to ask a harder question the chapter concept doesn't raise: whether a single project-specific opposition statement should stay a one-off, or whether the wider Côa Valley movement should generalise it into a standing policy position. A companion note, `integrated-infrastructure-proposal-coa-valley.md`, picks up where this one stops and sets out one concrete direction that generalisation could take.

## What Rewilding Portugal actually said

The source is a saved screenshot of a Rewilding Portugal news post, published 18 November 2025, titled "'If the price to pay is biodiversity itself, don't call it green energy'", subtitled "Official position of Rewilding Portugal on the SOPHIA Project (Fundão–Penamacor Solar Power Plant) and associated LMATs [very high voltage lines]." SOPHIA sits in the Beira Interior, in the municipalities of Fundão, Penamacor and Idanha-a-Nova, near but outside the Côa Valley proper, the same wider region the Côa sits in rather than the same watershed.

The scale, per Rewilding Portugal's reading of the project's own Environmental Impact Assessment: a total footprint over 3,500 hectares, of which around 434 ha would be directly covered by panels, paths and associated infrastructure, plus two 400kV transmission lines, each about 22 km long, connecting to the Fundão substation. The area crosses the Serra da Gardunha Special Conservation Area (Natura 2000) and the Serra da Gardunha Regional Protected Landscape Area, and its southern boundary approaches the UNESCO Naturtejo World Geopark. Seven habitat types of high ecological value are documented there: evergreen Quercus montados, oak groves, willow groves, ash groves and alluvial forests. The EIA itself records 231 vertebrate species in the study area, 30 of them endangered, among them the black stork (*Ciconia nigra*), the Cinereous vulture (*Aegypius monachus*), the imperial eagle (*Aquila adalberti*) and *Bufonia macropetala*, a rare Iberian endemic plant. Construction would fell legally protected holm oaks and cork oaks; the proposed compensation, converting 135 ha of eucalyptus into 228 ha of holm and cork oak, is one Rewilding Portugal rejects as no net gain, on the grounds that a mature, functioning habitat isn't replaced by a planting scheme that takes decades to reach the same state. The technical detail that matters most for a Côa-connectivity reading of this: the project would cut across two ecological corridors designated "Raia Norte" and "Raia Sul" under PROF Centro Interior, the regional forest management plan, corridors whose function is exactly the wildlife movement this project's other desk notes track.

Rewilding Portugal structures its opposition on three pillars, biodiversity, communities and visitation, and it's worth being precise about which one carries the corridor argument. The biodiversity pillar states it directly: "Nature needs space and connectivity to thrive, and the SOPHIA project blatantly ignores this principle by installing a solar megastructure in a mosaic of habitats that functions as a regional ecological corridor, cutting off the continuity between the natural areas of Gardunha, Raia and Malcata, and thus creating a physical and ecological barrier that is difficult to reverse." The communities pillar makes an environmental-justice argument, that rural, sparsely populated Beira Interior is being asked to host the infrastructure cost of a national energy transition without a proportional share of its benefit, "perpetuating historical inequalities and contravening the principle of environmental justice that should prevail." The visitation pillar names the Rede Côa Selvagem, the same network of small nature-tourism and hospitality businesses (Planalto Dourado, Ambieduca, Wild River Tours and fifteen others, listed as co-signatories) whose livelihoods depend on the intact landscape SOPHIA would alter, some of them, like Grande Vale do Côa, operating close enough to feel the effect without sitting inside the project boundary itself.

The line that matters most for this note, because it's the one that sets up the harder question below, is the siting-conditional one. Rewilding Portugal is explicit that this is not a blanket rejection of solar power:

> "Rejecting SOPHIA does not mean rejecting solar energy. It means demanding responsible planning, transparency and ecological justice. There are many other areas that have already been artificialised, abandoned after human use or intervention, or even the roofs of public buildings and other strips of land, which would allow the production of this same energy without destroying habitats and creating a technological monoculture. We must have the courage to make these decisions, because large-scale solar power plants, if poorly located, replace living ecosystems with dead surfaces, creating ecological deserts in a country that needs to renaturalise, reconnect and restore itself."

And they name what they want instead of SOPHIA, not just what they oppose:

> "We therefore propose that the Government and the competent authorities promote: the mapping of artificial areas available for this type of installation; robust tax incentives for the installation of panels on public, logistics and industrial buildings; and the creation of a Nature-based Energy Transition programme, which ensures that each megawatt produced also contributes to restoring ecosystems, and is produced without jeopardising them."

The statement closes on the line the storyMap chapter title already borrows: "There is no possible mitigation for cutting an ecological corridor in half, creating new barriers to a nature that is already so obstructed and fragmented. If the price to pay is biodiversity itself, don't call it green energy."

## How this connects to the storyMap chapter

`storyMaps/5-ideas.md` sketches a chapter, "If the Price Is Biodiversity, Don't Call It Green — Ribeira do Mosteiro," under the heading "What breaks connectivity," and describes it as: "Frame Ribeira do Mosteiro as part of the same corridor network they describe being cut elsewhere, use their line about creating new barriers to nature that's already fragmented as the argument, then show an intact stretch through your own site." Having now read the source directly rather than working from the chapter concept's paraphrase, that framing holds up: the corridor language ("Raia Norte," "Raia Sul," continuity between Gardunha, Raia and Malcata) is genuinely about landscape-scale connectivity, not a generic environmental objection dressed up in connectivity language, and it sits comfortably alongside the "Who Moves Through the Côa" and "Water Without Borders" chapters as the same fragmentation argument applied to a different threat type, infrastructure siting rather than a river barrier. One correction for the drafting stage: SOPHIA is in Fundão–Penamacor, not in the Côa watershed itself, so the chapter's move from "Rewilding Portugal's SOPHIA opposition" to "Ribeira do Mosteiro" needs to be explicit that it's an analogy across the same regional corridor network and the same organisation's stated principles, not a claim that SOPHIA directly threatens the Ribeira do Mosteiro site.

## The harder question: one statement, or a standing position?

The SOPHIA statement is a response to one project. It was triggered by a specific EIA, a specific public consultation window, and a specific set of co-signatories with a direct stake in that stretch of the Beira Interior. Nothing in it commits Rewilding Portugal, or the wider Côa Valley rewilding and conservation-tourism movement, to a standing position on renewable energy siting generally. That raises a real strategic choice the movement hasn't had to make yet, at least not in anything I found in this pass: keep responding project by project as each new SOPHIA-shaped proposal surfaces, or generalise the siting-conditional logic already stated here, artificialised land first, rooftops and brownfield before greenfield, corridors off the table, into a proactive policy position the movement advocates for regardless of which specific project is in front of it.

There's a live national process this question could actually plug into right now. Portugal is transposing the EU's third Renewable Energy Directive (RED III), which requires member states to designate "renewables acceleration areas," informally "go-to areas," regions with high renewable potential and lower environmental risk where permitting is deliberately fast-tracked, down to as little as six months inside a go-to area against up to two years (plus a six-month extension) outside one. RED III's own text instructs member states to prioritise artificial and built surfaces, rooftops, transport infrastructure corridors, parking areas, industrial sites, mines and degraded agricultural land, when identifying those areas. Portugal's version, the Zonas de Aceleração para Energias Renováveis (ZAER), branded publicly as the "Mapa Verde" or Green Map, went to public consultation in June 2026, built by an independent technical team led by Maria do Rosário Partidário on criteria of environmental sensitivity, energy potential, territorial planning and grid proximity. The draft decree-law reportedly excludes the Natura 2000 network and other protected areas from future acceleration zones outright, and steers projects toward urban, artificialised or degraded land, which is close to word-for-word what Rewilding Portugal asked for in the SOPHIA statement nine months earlier. Ambiente Magazine's environmental reporting on the consultation is less optimistic, describing Portugal as still lacking a clear, robust strategy for prioritising which zones actually get designated, which is exactly the gap between a stated principle and an enforced one that a case-by-case advocacy model risks leaving open indefinitely.

Other jurisdictions offer three different shapes this proactive stance could take, none of them a perfect template, each with a documented weakness:

(i) The Netherlands built a formal "zonneladder" (solar ladder) in 2020, a strict order of preference: rooftops and façades first, vacant or brownfield land in urban areas second, rural land third, agricultural and natural land last and to be limited as much as possible. Nearly every Dutch province and many municipalities have written some version of it into local policy. The documented failure mode is enforcement: research on its implementation found the ladder under-used in practice, with unclear regulations, absent spatial-planning frameworks and no binding instrument from national government, at almost every step. A well-designed ladder without a legal floor under it is a statement of intent, not a constraint on where solar actually gets built.

(ii) Germany has run spatial control over ground-mounted solar (*Freiflächenanlagen*) through its Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG) since 2004, steering installations toward ecologically and agriculturally lower-value land, with a standing privileged corridor of 200 m along motorways and certain rail lines, roughly 930,000 ha of pre-screened land once protected areas are excluded. The 2024 Solar Package I reforms extended this further, adding agrivoltaics provisions for dual land use. This is the most institutionally mature of the three, decades of continuous policy rather than a single directive, but it's also the hardest to transplant into Portugal's much smaller, less centralised planning apparatus in the time SOPHIA-style projects are already moving through.

(iii) RED III itself, and Portugal's own Mapa Verde process now underway, are the EU-level and national-level version of the same idea, arriving after both the Dutch and German examples and visibly drawing on the same brownfield-first logic they established.

None of this settles the strategic question, it just gives it real shape. Case-by-case opposition, the SOPHIA statement's own model, has an immediate, concrete advantage: it can stop a specific bad project on a specific timeline, using that project's own EIA data, and it mobilises a specific, motivated coalition, seventeen named Rede Côa Selvagem businesses with a direct financial stake in this one outcome. A standing policy position is slower by construction, it means sustained technical engagement in processes like the Mapa Verde consultation, months of comment periods and criteria-setting rather than a single news post, and the Dutch case shows a well-articulated priority ladder can still fail to bind if nobody enforces it. But the case-by-case model has its own cost, paid in repetition: every SOPHIA-shaped proposal that surfaces in the Beira Interior or the wider Côa region has to be fought again, from the same EIA-reading, same-coalition-building starting point, unless something upstream of individual projects, a binding go-to-areas map that actually excludes corridors like Raia Norte and Raia Sul, changes where developers can propose projects in the first place. Whether the Côa Valley movement should spend its limited advocacy capacity on the next SOPHIA, or on making sure the Mapa Verde consultation now open actually encodes what the SOPHIA statement already argued, is the open question this note is raising rather than resolving.
