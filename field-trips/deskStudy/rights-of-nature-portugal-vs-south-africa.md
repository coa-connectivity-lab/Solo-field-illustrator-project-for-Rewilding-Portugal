# Rights of nature, Portugal vs. South Africa — and why that gap matters for the Côa Valley's missing wildlife

Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 22 August 2026, desk research for `coa_eco_connectivity_story_v4.ipynb`

This is desk research, not a field visit: no site was walked for this note. It exists to extend one specific subsection of v3 of the eco-connectivity notebook — "Limpopo, South Africa — strategic lessons for expanding the Greater Côa Valley initiative," and within that, the "Water governance: a binding ecological Reserve vs. a stalled protocol" comparison (`research/eco-connectivity/notebook/coa_eco_connectivity_story_v3.ipynb`). v3 names South Africa's National Water Act Reserve as "a meaningfully stronger legal instrument than anything currently governing the Côa's own water" and leaves it there, as one line in a summary table. This note goes further on two questions v3 doesn't ask: precisely what kind of legal mechanism the Reserve actually is (a question that turns out to matter — see below), and whether Portugal or the EU has anything comparable at all. It then uses that legal gap to help explain a fact this project's own field notes keep circling around without quite naming: the Greater Côa Valley, for most of the 20th century and into the 21st, has had next to no wildlife.

## What South Africa's Reserve actually grants

South Africa's National Water Act, 1998 (Act 36 of 1998) created "the Reserve" in its Chapter 3, and it is worth being precise about what that word covers, because it is doing two different jobs at once. The Reserve has two components: a **basic human needs reserve** (water for drinking, food preparation, and personal hygiene) and an **ecological reserve** (water required to protect the aquatic ecosystems of the resource itself — both its quantity and its quality). Once the Minister determines the Reserve for a given water resource, it is legally binding in the same way as the resource's class and quality objectives, and the Minister, the Director-General, every organ of state, and every water management institution are obliged to give effect to it. Multiple legal-scholarship sources describe the Reserve as being afforded the *highest* priority in South African water allocation — it cannot itself be allocated away for irrigation, industrial, mining, or any other licensed use; those uses are allocated only out of what remains.

The Act was written to correct a specific, named historical wrong. Under apartheid, the white minority held roughly 87% of the land and the water infrastructure that came with it; by 1994, in the words of one of the scholarly sources below, "hardly any Black individual had a formal water resource right, not even for just basic needs." The National Water Act rejected the riparian-rights doctrine inherited from colonial and apartheid law, made water a resource held in public trust by the state rather than a private entitlement attached to land ownership, and named equity and redress as explicit statutory objectives — not incidental effects.

Two qualifications belong here, in keeping with this project's own habit of not overclaiming:

- **This is a minimum-allocation guarantee, not legal personhood.** The comparative rights-of-nature literature treats Ecuador's 2008 constitution (which grants rights to *Pachamama* and lets any citizen sue on nature's behalf — a public-guardianship model) and New Zealand's Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017 (which declares the river itself a legal person, with human-appointed guardians acting on its behalf in court) as the paradigm cases of rights-of-nature law. Those instruments make the natural entity itself a rights-holder with standing. South Africa's Reserve does not do that: the river or aquifer is not a party who can sue: the state — the Minister and the water management institutions — is the party legally obliged to protect a defined minimum flow on the ecosystem's behalf. Searching specifically for scholarly work that classifies the Reserve alongside Ecuador/Whanganui-style personhood regimes turned up nothing — this looks like a genuinely under-examined comparison rather than a settled one, and the more defensible characterization is that the Reserve is **a statutory minimum-allocation mechanism that functions like a right to water for ecosystems**, not that South Africa has granted its rivers legal personhood.
- **The law being strong on paper hasn't closed the gap it was built to close.** Multiple post-apartheid water-governance reviews (cited below) report that, decades on, "the biggest users of water remain white commercial farmers" and that allocations to smallholder farmers have not meaningfully increased. The Reserve is real, binding law, with a real ecological-flow guarantee inside it — but its equity mandate is still substantially unrealized in practice. Worth holding both facts at once rather than resolving them into either "South Africa solved this" or "the law is meaningless."

## Portugal and the EU: essentially nothing comparable exists

This is genuinely new ground for this project — nothing in the repo currently touches rights-of-nature or ecological water-allocation law for Portugal or the EU. The finding, after searching directly, is that the asymmetry v3 gestured at is close to total.

**Portugal**: no constitutional provision, statute, or case law granting legal personhood or a binding minimum-allocation guarantee to any river, ecosystem, or natural entity turned up in any search. Portugal does not appear on any of the several "which countries grant rights to nature" survey lists checked for this note (Ecuador, Bolivia, New Zealand, India/Bangladesh court rulings, and a scattering of municipal/river-specific charters like England's River Wye Charter are the entries that do appear). The closest things Portugal has are Natura 2000 site designations and the EU Water Framework Directive's "good status" objectives — and as v3 already notes, the Water Framework Directive layer in this project's own pipeline (`data/processed/hydrology_and_protected_areas.gpkg`) is *descriptive*: it sets a water-quality target and reports against it, but it does not create a binding minimum-flow guarantee the way South Africa's ecological Reserve does. Natura 2000 protects habitat extent and designated species, not water quantity as such.

**The EU**: no directive, regulation, or binding instrument exists either, but there is active movement toward one. A coalition first proposed a draft EU directive on rights of nature back in 2017 (the "Nature's Rights" initiative), which was never adopted. More recently, in 2026, the European Commission formally registered a new European Citizens' Initiative, "Rights for Nature: Empower Citizens to Represent and Protect Ecosystems," which asks the Commission to recognise ecosystems as rights-holders under EU law — explicitly framed by its organisers as a shift "from treating nature as property to recognising ecosystems as living entities with intrinsic value and fundamental rights." As of this note, it is at the signature-collection stage (starting autumn 2026, needing one million statements of support across at least seven member states) — a live proposal, not law, and with no guarantee of adoption even if it clears that bar.

So the honest statement is the plain one: Portugal and the EU currently have no legal mechanism resembling South Africa's Reserve, in either its personhood sense or its minimum-allocation sense. The one concrete indicator of how far that gap reaches locally: Faia Brava, the first private reserve inside what's now the Greater Côa Valley initiative, is described in multiple sources as **Portugal's only private protected area** — a fact that is really a statement about what state or EU-level legal protection *doesn't* reach, not just a fact about one reserve's founding.

## Why the Greater Côa Valley has next to no wildlife

"Next to no wildlife" needs its own qualification before the history: it is not literally zero, and the direction over the last few years is recovery, not further decline. This project's own data shows real, if sparse, presence — the European beaver, confirmed present in the Greater Côa Valley for the first time in "more than 500 years," via natural dispersal from Spain, first confirmed in 2025 (`references/ecological-information/data/annual_review_2025_species.csv`); cinereous vultures holding 15 nests in Serra da Malcata; griffon and Egyptian vultures, Bonelli's and golden eagles, and black storks logged as regularly present at Faia Brava (`references/ecological-information/data/visitor_board_species_2026.csv`); and the Iberian wolf confirmed at Ermo das Águias under the LIFE WolfLux project, per a colleague's August 2026 field confirmation in the same visitor-board dataset. But a meaningful share of what's present is either a managed reintroduction (the Sorraia horse and Tauros herds are semi-wild grazers released by the rewilding project, not wild recolonizers) or a species whose local presence depends on active cross-border monitoring and dispersal from Spain, not a self-sustaining recovered baseline. The wolf entry itself names a EU LIFE-funded project as the reason it's trackable here at all.

The scarcity this recovery is climbing out of has several compounding, well-documented drivers:

**Deforestation and land-use intensification, then abandonment.** Sources describing the valley's mid-20th-century state call it "virtually treeless" 60–70 years ago — forest cleared over generations for fuel, agricultural land, and charcoal to smelt iron ore. Then, from the 1980s on, the opposite pressure took over: Portugal's small, fragmented interior farms (fields under half a hectare) couldn't compete with industrialized agriculture after EU accession in 1986, and Common Agricultural Policy support consistently concentrated in the country's more prosperous, irrigated coastal and lowland regions, leaving the poorer, more forested interior — where the Côa sits — with comparatively little CAP support. The resulting pattern, named directly in the land-use literature as "polarization," is intensification in the fertile areas and abandonment in the marginal ones, simultaneously accelerating biodiversity loss, wildfire exposure, and rural depopulation in exactly the interior region this project works in. The Côa Valley specifically is cited as having some of the highest rural-abandonment rates in Europe.

**Predator extirpation, driven by direct, sustained persecution.** The Iberian wolf occupied at least 65% of the Iberian Peninsula as recently as the mid-19th century; systematic poisoning campaigns beginning in the first half of the 1800s and intensifying through the mid-20th century drove the peninsula-wide population down to roughly 400–500 animals by the 1970s. In Portugal specifically, the wolf's range contracted to the northern half of the country, having lost its entire southern range over the 20th century — it now occupies only about 20% of its historical Portuguese distribution. One of the few areas where a population persisted through that collapse is named directly in the literature as the area just south of the Douro River in central Portugal — the Côa Valley's own doorstep. This is not ancient history: it is the direct, documented reason wolf presence in the valley today depends on an EU LIFE project rather than an intact, continuous population.

**Hunting as a current, not just historical, land use.** This project's own land-tenure data (`research/eco-connectivity/data/processed/land_tenure.gpkg`, sourced from ICNF's hunting-zone cadastre) returns 337 hunting zones inside the catchment-extended study area — a major, currently-active land use across the valley, not a closed chapter. Consistent with this project's own established convention (v3's land-tenure section, and the Faia Brava field notes), this note does not model or assert how any individual zone is actually managed; the point being made here is narrower and purely structural: hunting is a legally sanctioned land use operating across a large share of the valley right now, and, unlike South Africa's water Reserve, nothing in Portuguese or EU law subordinates that use to a prioritized ecological minimum. There is no equivalent of "the Reserve comes first, hunting-zone licensing gets what's left."

**And no legal floor under recovery — only voluntary purchase.** This is the direct tie back to Part 1. South Africa, however imperfectly it has delivered on the promise in practice, has a binding statutory mechanism that puts ecological need ahead of extractive/productive water use across the whole country by default. Portugal has nothing structurally equivalent for water, land, or species. Every gain logged in this project's own data — Faia Brava's existence as Portugal's only private protected area, the 997 hectares Rewilding Portugal has purchased toward its 5,000-hectare-by-2030 target (per v3, sourced from the Endangered Landscapes & Seascapes Programme), the Ermo das Águias horse and Tauros releases, even the stalled cofferdam-removal protocol v3 already flags as "awaiting approval" three years on — has happened because a private philanthropic actor voluntarily bought land or funded a project, not because any Portuguese or EU law required it. The beaver's return is the one exception worth naming honestly: that happened on its own, by natural dispersal from Spain, independent of any legal mechanism on either side of the border. Everything else the recovery has depended on is either voluntary funding or an accident of cross-border ecology — there is no Portuguese or EU equivalent of "the ecological Reserve comes first," and that absence is a large part of why recovery here has been this slow, this partial, and this dependent on one organisation's continued ability to keep buying land.
