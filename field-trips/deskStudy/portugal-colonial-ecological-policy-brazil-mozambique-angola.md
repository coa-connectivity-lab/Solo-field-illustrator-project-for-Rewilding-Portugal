Colonial water and land policy: ecological legacies in Brazil, Mozambique, and Angola
Linda Angulo Lopez, compiled 23 August 2026, desk research for the "Water, Empire, and Portugal's Environmental Legacy" closing section, read through the lens established in `safe-and-just-indigenous-science-earth-system-justice.md`

This note treats Portugal's colonial-era environmental footprint in Brazil, Mozambique, and Angola as a genuine part of this study's water-and-land throughline, not a detour from it. It reads that history through the corrected framing this project now uses: Indigenous Science and community-led resistance as the standpoint that names what colonial and extractive policy actually did, not a neutral state-to-state comparison of environmental regulations.

## Brazil: coastal devastation, interior neglect, then a policy reversal

Portuguese colonization of Brazil (1500-1822) followed a striking geographic split. European settlers began clearing the Atlantic Forest almost immediately after arriving on Brazil's eastern coast in the 16th century, exploitation peaking through the 19th and 20th centuries. The Amazon interior, by contrast, remained largely unsettled through the entire colonial period: settlement concentrated on Atlantic-coast cash crops, principally sugar, in the northeast, leaving the Amazon basin peripheral to sporadic expeditions and river outposts rather than sustained clearing (per general environmental-history accounts of Portuguese Brazil). Two different colonial ecological legacies from one colony: the coast paid the cost first, the interior's reckoning came later, and arguably continues now.

Post-independence, Brazil's environmental lawmaking began with the Forest Codes of 1934 and 1967, the latter creating the "command and control" rule obliging landowners to keep a share of their property under native vegetation. The 1988 Constitution made environmental protection a citizen's right and a state duty. In 2004, Brazil launched PPCDAm (the Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Deforestation in the Amazon), pairing satellite monitoring with blacklists and credit restrictions; deforestation fell from 27,772 km² in 2004 to 4,571 km² by 2012. The most recent reporting shows continued improvement: 5,796 km² lost in the Legal Amazon in the year to July 2025, the lowest rate in 11 years, and DETER alerts down 36% the following year, the lowest since 2013 (both figures and sources already documented in `safe-and-just-social-foundations-planetary-boundaries.md`). That is a genuine, state-led policy success story, not an unqualified one: the same period saw a rising share of loss from fire-associated "progressive degradation" rather than clear-cutting, and a 2024 drought that alone destroyed 2.78 million hectares of primary forest.

## Mozambique: from colonial extraction to a present-day land fight that echoes it directly

Portuguese rule in Mozambique (formally to 1975) followed the same broad extraction-for-the-metropole pattern documented across the empire. What makes Mozambique's entry in this note distinctive is not the colonial history itself but a present-day case that echoes it with unusual directness: **ProSavana**, a Brazil-Japan-Mozambique agribusiness and land development project centred on the Nacala Corridor, one of Mozambique's most populated areas and home to one of its last remaining tropical forest tracts. Critics documented ProSavana as risking the largest land-grab in the country's history, threatening roughly four million small-scale farmers with displacement in favour of soy and cash-crop export agriculture aimed at the Japanese market ([Global Forest Coalition, "The Biggest Land Grabbing in Africa - PROSAVANNA"](https://globalforestcoalition.org/the-bisggest-land-grabbing-in-africa-prosavanna/)).

Resistance to ProSavana was community-led and cross-border: Mozambique's **União Nacional de Camponeses (UNAC)**, the national peasants' union, and **Justiça Ambiental (JA!)**, Mozambique's Friends of the Earth affiliate, organised alongside Brazil's own **Movimento dos Trabalhadores Rurais Sem Terra (MST)**, the Landless Workers' Movement, joined by Friends of the Earth International, in a campaign that ran more than five years before the project's contested master plan was substantially curtailed ([Truthout, "Mozambique's Movement to End Land Grabs"](https://truthout.org/articles/mozambique-s-movement-to-end-land-grabs/); [IDS, "Agricultural development in Mozambique since the contestations around ProSAVANA"](https://www.ids.ac.uk/opinions/agricultural-development-in-mozambique-since-the-contestations-around-prosavana/)). The detail worth naming plainly: this is a Brazilian social movement standing with Mozambican peasants against a Brazilian agribusiness interest, in a former Portuguese colony, on land whose colonial-era extraction logic (export crops over subsistence farming, foreign capital over local tenure) the ProSavana project substantially reproduced. Community-led resistance, not state policy, is what actually constrained it, precisely the standpoint this project's corrected "safe and just" framing centres.

## Angola: a promising late-colonial conservation start, then total collapse, then extraction repeating

Angola's environmental-policy arc under Portuguese rule looks, on paper, more progressive than its neighbours': a conservation fund financed by hunting-licence sales was established in 1911, followed by natural parks and game reserves through the 1930s, and a marked expansion of conservation effort in the early 1970s, including a scientific advisor appointed in 1971 to assess the existing protected-area network and propose its expansion ("[Conservation in Angola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_in_Angola)"). By independence in 1975, roughly 6% of Angola's land area was officially protected.

That progress did not survive contact with war. The outbreak of Angola's post-independence civil war saw conservation efforts functionally abandoned; formerly protected areas ceased to be managed at all. In the vacuum, corporate interests around diamonds and oil flourished amid weak post-colonial governance, continuing colonial extraction logics under new ownership rather than displacing them (per general environmental-history accounts of post-independence Angola). The pattern worth naming: colonial conservation infrastructure did not fail because the idea was wrong, it failed because war and weak governance removed the capacity to run it, and the extractive economy that filled the resulting vacuum was the colonial economy's direct successor, not its opposite.

Angola's coastal ecology today sits inside the Benguela Current system documented in `portugal-land-to-sea-ocean-currents-colonial-routes.md`, a live climate-vulnerability case, not a settled one.

## What ties these three together, and what does not

Brazil, Mozambique, and Angola do not share one colonial ecological story. Brazil's is a coast-versus-interior split resolved, imperfectly, by strong post-independence state institutions. Mozambique's is a colonial extraction pattern still being actively resisted today, by name, by the same kind of community organising this project's corrected framing insists on centring. Angola's is a promising start destroyed by war and reoccupied by extraction. What does tie them together: in all three, the standpoint that actually explains what happened to the land and water is not the colonial administration's own environmental record, gestural in Angola's case, negligent in Brazil's interior, extractive throughout, but the record of who resisted, rebuilt, or is still fighting for a different relationship with that land, exactly the standpoint Indigenous Science and community-led climate justice movements hold, per `safe-and-just-indigenous-science-earth-system-justice.md`.
