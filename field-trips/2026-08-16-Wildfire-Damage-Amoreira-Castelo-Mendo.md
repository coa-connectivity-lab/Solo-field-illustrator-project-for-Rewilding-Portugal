## The fire itself

Per SIC Notícias and other coverage from 28–29 July 2026 (see References): a wildfire started around 13:51 on 27 July near Rochoso, in Guarda municipality, and spread into the neighbouring municipalities of **Almeida, Sabugal, and Pinhel**. It burned for two days before being brought under control overnight into Wednesday 29 July, by which point it had consumed **more than 5,000 hectares**. At the peak of the response, over 400 personnel and multiple aircraft were deployed; four firefighters were injured, not seriously. The A25 motorway and Estrada Nacional 16 were both closed for several hours due to flames and heavy smoke, reopening on the 29th. In Freixo (Almeida municipality), one livestock producer alone lost roughly 100 hectares of pasture that had been feeding about 40 animals — the kind of concrete loss that a hectare count doesn't convey on its own.

This is almost certainly the same fire responsible for the scorched boulders and dead shrub growth logged as an unconfirmed observation in the Vale das Éguas note two days later — that note flagged fire scarring as "inferred... not confirmed by any burn-date source." It's now confirmed: a 5,000+ hectare fire burned through Almeida municipality (among others) 27–29 July, well within reach of both sites on this route.

## Habitat description

The two photos show visibly different vegetation types within what reads as the same general burn:

- One frame is inside a scorched pine stand, most likely maritime pine (*Pinus pinaster*, cf., the dominant plantation/naturalised conifer on these acid soils), trunks blackened, canopy needles gone from orange-brown to fully scorched rather than green, ground reduced to grey-white ash with charred fallen branches and what look like burst aerosol/spray cans among the debris. The still-attached, browned (not yet fallen) needle canopy is a useful severity read on its own: it means the fire scorched and killed the crown without necessarily consuming it outright, consistent with roughly two to three weeks' regrowth lag by the time of the photo.
- The other frame is open ground: bare granite/schist boulders exposed on a blackened slope, with the charred skeletons of low shrubs, plus pale, bleached-looking resprouting stems already pushing up through the ash in the foreground, a first visible sign of post-fire recovery. This is the same rock-and-scrub terrain type as the "savanna-like" upland photographed at Vale das Éguas the same day, strengthening the case that both stops sit inside the same fire's footprint rather than being coincidentally similar.
