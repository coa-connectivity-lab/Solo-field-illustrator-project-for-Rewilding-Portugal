# Story Maps, reorganised around eco-connectivity

The five ideas already map onto your own three-dimension framework, longitudinal, lateral/riparian, terrestrial, rather than needing new material. This version groups them by connectivity type instead of by site and date, with a framing chapter opening the series and the political comparison closing it.

## Opening frame

**Three Centuries of a Frontier — Almeida**
Not a connectivity dimension itself, the premise underneath all three. Opens on the 1766 fortress map, a place built to control terrain and movement, pivots to the same valley managed today for wildlife movement instead. Establishes why connectivity is the right lens before the reader sees any of it applied. Already substantially in hand, desk-heavy from here.

## Longitudinal connectivity (aquatic)

**Water Without Borders — Almeida/Côa and Paul de Toirões**
Two water stories under one connectivity type, flowing and standing. The river side: Pontão Manuel José as the small barrier case, the Open Rivers Programme's Ensecadeiras do Côa dam removal as the large one, same fragmentation theme at two scales, closing on the verified fish list (*Unio crassus*, *Anguilla anguilla*, *Chondrostoma polylepis*, *Squalius alburnoides*, *Rutilus arcasii*) and the riparian corridor. Paul de Toirões adds the wetland case once you've been, standing water and marsh connectivity as the counterpoint to a flowing channel. Feeds `04_resistance_surface_aquatic.ipynb` directly.

## Terrestrial connectivity (structural/functional)

**Who Moves Through the Côa — Vale Carapito**
The movement dimension: Iberian wolf, griffon and Egyptian vulture, roe and red deer, drawn from the verified Endangered Landscapes list, grounded by your own grazer ID and dung beetle field work under the 23 Sorraia horses and 17 Tauros cattle reintroduction numbers. Opens on the headline figures, moves to what natural grazing looks like up close, closes on the prey-base argument for wolf recovery. Needs the most source-checking, keep the ELSP figures clearly separate from what you actually observed.

## What breaks connectivity

**If the Price Is Biodiversity, Don't Call It Green — Ribeira do Mosteiro**
The threat case, corridor fragmentation made explicit through Rewilding Portugal's SOPHIA opposition piece. Frame Ribeira do Mosteiro as part of the same corridor network they describe being cut elsewhere, use their line about creating new barriers to nature that's already fragmented as the argument, then show an intact stretch through your own site. This is where the first three chapters' connectivity actually earns its stakes, shown against what's lost when it's cut.

## Closing frame, the political dimension

**Two Rivers, Two Stories — Porto, 20-21 August**
Connectivity as political choice, not just ecological fact. The Douro, 141 dams and weirs, extensively regulated for hydroelectric power and navigation, a UNESCO wine landscape built on controlling the river's flow. The Côa, a 1990s dam stopped by civil society to save the rock art, and the still-open Ensecadeiras do Côa removal case today. Two rivers a few kilometres apart, one embraced for damming, one that fought it. Closes the series by showing that the connectivity you've spent four chapters documenting was, and still is, a choice someone had to make. Dam counts and political detail on both rivers still need verifying against a primary source before drafting.
